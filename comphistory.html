Computer science · Middle school guide

Margaret Hamilton wrote the rules that keep computers from panicking.

In 1969 she led the team that wrote the software flying Apollo 11 to the Moon. Her code was so carefully planned that when the computer got overloaded three minutes before landing, it calmly dropped the unimportant jobs, kept the important ones, and let the astronauts land.

She also gave her job a name: software engineering. Today millions of people have that job title, and the ideas she used are inside your phone, the school's computers, and the cash register at the grocery store.

Step by step

Her life in order

These steps happened in this order. Tap any "More" button for the longer story.

  1. 1936–1958

    Step 1: A curious kid who loved math

    Margaret grew up in Indiana and Michigan. In college she studied math and also philosophy, which is the study of how to think clearly and argue logically. Both turned out to matter for writing computer code.

  2. 1959–1963

    Step 2: First coding jobs: weather and air defense

    She took a job at MIT writing programs to predict the weather. Then she wrote software for SAGE, a giant system that watched the sky for enemy airplanes. There was no "coding class" back then. She learned by doing.

  3. Mid-1960s

    Step 3: Joining the Moon project

    NASA asked MIT to build the computer that would guide the Apollo spacecraft. Margaret joined and became the leader of the team writing the "onboard flight software," the code that ran inside the spaceship.

  4. July 20, 1969

    Step 4: Apollo 11 lands on the Moon

    During the landing, alarms went off. Her software handled the overload and the landing went on. Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin walked on the Moon.

    Read the landing story

  5. 1976–today

    Step 5: Starting companies and new ideas

    She started her own software companies. She studied every error from Apollo and created a way to design systems so many mistakes can't happen in the first place.

  6. 2003 & 2016

    Step 6: Big honors

    NASA gave her a special award in 2003. In 2016 President Obama gave her the Presidential Medal of Freedom, the highest honor a regular citizen can get in the United States.

Background

First, some words you need

Before her story makes sense, let's get clear on a few ideas. Each one has a grocery store or school example.

Hardware vs. software

Hardware is the physical machine you can touch. Software is the list of instructions that tells the machine what to do.

Grocery store: The scanner and screen at checkout are hardware. The program that looks up "bananas = $0.59 a pound" and adds it to your total is software.

A program

A program is a recipe for a computer. It must be exact, because a computer does only what it is told, in the order it is told.

School: Your morning schedule is a program: homeroom, then math, then science. If the bell schedule had a typo, everyone would end up in the wrong room.

A bug

A bug is a mistake in a program. Some bugs are small. In a spaceship, a bug could be deadly.

Grocery store: If the price list said milk costs $40 instead of $4, every customer would be charged wrong until someone fixed it.

The Apollo Guidance Computer

The computer in the Apollo spacecraft was about the size of a carry-on suitcase. Your phone has well over a million times more storage.

Why was this so hard in the 1960s?

  • Nobody had ever written software that people's lives depended on in space.
  • There were no schools for coding, no internet to look things up, and no "undo" button.
  • The final program was stored by literally weaving copper wire through tiny magnetic rings. Changing it once it was woven meant re-weaving.
  • Many people thought software was a small side job, less "real" than building rockets.

Her contributions

Six ideas we still use

Each card explains the idea in plain words, then gives an everyday example. Tap "More" for details and official links.

Idea · Name it

"Software engineering"

She called her work software engineering so people would treat it as seriously as building bridges or rockets. At first people joked about it. Now it's a real job and a college major.

School: Calling it "the science fair" instead of "a poster thing" tells everyone to take it seriously and follow real steps.

Idea · Priorities

Do the most important job first

Every task in the Apollo software got a priority number. When the computer was too busy, it ran the highest-priority tasks and let the low ones wait or go.

Grocery store: On a busy Saturday, the cashier keeps scanning and taking payment. Restocking the gum rack waits until later.

Idea · Recovery

Expect trouble and recover

Her team planned for things to go wrong. If the computer got confused, it could restart in a split second and pick up the important work where it left off.

School: Google Docs auto-saves. If your Chromebook freezes, you restart and your essay is still there.

Idea · Tell the human

Warn people clearly

When something went wrong, the computer interrupted the astronauts' screen with an alarm code so humans could decide what to do.

Grocery store: Self-checkout flashes "Unexpected item in bagging area" and calls a worker instead of silently charging you wrong.

Idea · Testing

Test everything, even "impossible" mistakes

Her young daughter pushed the wrong buttons on a simulator and crashed it. Margaret realized a real astronaut could do the same thing. Later, one did.

School: A good quiz app checks what happens if a student types letters in a number box, not just correct answers.

Idea · Design it right

Stop errors before they start

After Apollo, she studied all the bugs and found most came from parts not fitting together. She built a method to design systems so those mistakes can't be made.

Grocery store: A gas pump nozzle for diesel is a different size, so it won't fit in a regular car. The mistake is blocked by design.

The big moment · July 20, 1969

The 1202 alarm, step by step

This is the most famous test her software ever faced. The steps happened in this order, over just a few minutes.

  1. 01

    The lunar module Eagle starts its descent to the Moon. The computer is steering and controlling the engine.

  2. 02

    A radar switch is in a position that makes the radar keep "poking" the computer with signals it doesn't need. This steals part of the computer's time.

  3. 03

    The computer now has more work than it can finish. It flashes 1202, and later 1201. These codes mean "I'm overloaded."

  4. 04

    Because of the priority design, the computer restarts quickly, throws out low-priority jobs, and keeps the jobs needed for landing.

  5. 05

    In Mission Control, the experts know this alarm is safe as long as it doesn't keep happening nonstop. They tell the crew "Go."

  6. 06

    Armstrong flies the last part by hand to avoid a field of boulders. Eagle lands safely.

    Math & logic

    The math under her code

    Margaret studied math, and computer code is really math and logic written as instructions. Here are four ideas she used every day.

    1. Binary: counting with on and off

    Computers only understand two states: on (1) and off (0). Each spot is worth double the one to its right: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128. Tap the boxes to turn them on.

    Binary 00000000 = 0

    Grocery store: A barcode is black and white stripes, which is basically 1s and 0s. The scanner reads them and turns them into a product number.

    2. Logic: AND, OR, NOT

    Computers make decisions using rules called Boolean logic. Try it with a school rule: "We go outside for recess IF homework is done ___ it's not raining."

    AND needs both true. OR needs at least one. NOT flips true and false, so "not raining" is NOT(raining).

    3. If-then rules and checking every case

    Code is full of "IF this happens, THEN do that." Margaret's big lesson: you must also plan for "what if something unexpected happens?"

    IF item has a barcode
  THEN look up the price
ELSE IF item is produce
  THEN weigh it
ELSE
  THEN call a worker   ← the "just in case" rule

    School: IF it's a school day THEN take the bus. ELSE IF it's a snow day THEN stay home. ELSE (something weird, like a power outage) check the school's message.

    4. Ordering and percentages

    Priority scheduling is sorting plus adding. Sort the jobs from most to least important, then add up how much time each one needs until you hit 100%. Whatever doesn't fit waits.

    JobPriorityTimeRunning total

    Control engine225%65% ✓

    Systems thinking

    Seeing the whole picture

    A system is a group of parts that work together to do a job. Margaret didn't only think about her code. She thought about the astronauts, the hardware, Mission Control, and how they all connect. That is systems thinking.

    PrincipleWhat it meansGrocery storeSchool
    PartsName every piece of the system.Shelves, cashiers, scanners, trucks, customers.Students, teachers, buses, bells, cafeteria.
    ConnectionsLook at how parts affect each other.If the truck is late, shelves go empty, customers leave.If the bus is late, first period starts late for many kids.
    InterfacesWhere two parts meet, make the hand-off clear.The barcode is the "handshake" between the product and the scanner.A hall pass is the hand-off between two teachers.
    FeedbackThe system watches results and adjusts.Inventory computers see milk selling fast and order more.Quiz scores tell the teacher what to re-teach.
    Plan for failureAsk "what if this part breaks?" before it does.Backup generator keeps freezers cold in a power outage.Fire drills practice what to do in an emergency.
    Humans are part of itPeople make mistakes, so design for them.Self-checkout asks "Are you sure?" before removing an item.Online tests warn you before you submit with blank answers.

    Practice: think like Margaret about your school lunch line

    1. List the parts: students, trays, food stations, the cashier, the ID card reader.
    2. Draw the connections: students go to stations, then to the cashier, then to tables.
    3. Find the bottleneck: where does the line get stuck? Usually at the cashier.
    4. Set priorities: what must happen (paying, getting food) versus what can wait (choosing a dessert).
    5. Plan for failure: what if the card reader breaks? Have a paper list as a backup.
    6. Get feedback: time the line for a week and see if your changes helped.

    Her impact

    What works today because of ideas like hers

    Margaret didn't invent every one of these things. But the ideas her team proved on Apollo, like priorities, recovery, clear alarms, and serious testing, are built into them.

    Your phone

    When you get a call during a game, the phone pauses the game and rings. That's priority scheduling.

    Airplanes

    Autopilot and flight computers use priority tasks, backups, and clear cockpit alarms so pilots can react fast.

    Cars

    Airbag and brake computers must react in a fraction of a second, no matter what the radio or screen is doing.

    Grocery checkout

    Registers keep working even when the network hiccups, then catch up later. Error messages call a worker.

    School computers

    Auto-save, "Are you sure?" pop-ups, and crash recovery protect your homework from mistakes.

    Hospitals

    Heart monitors put urgent alarms ahead of everything else, the same idea as the 1202 priority display.

    Space today

    Modern rockets and Mars rovers run flight software built with the same care: priorities, testing, and fault recovery.

    A whole career

    "Software engineer" is now one of the most common tech jobs in the world, a name she helped make respected.

    Women in tech

    A famous 1969 photo shows her next to a stack of code printouts as tall as she was. It inspires students today.
