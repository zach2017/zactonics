# The Corinthian Table
### A Six-Session Study for Church Leaders on Discipline, Standards, Grace, Team Dynamics, and Cognitive Dissonance in 1 Corinthians

---

## Front Matter

### The thesis of this study

Paul writes to a congregation that is simultaneously gifted and fracturing. Corinth has spiritual power (1:5–7) and social rot (1:11; 5:1; 6:1; 11:21) in the same room. Paul's response is neither to lower the standard nor to abandon the people. He does something harder: he grounds every demand in an already-given grace, names the fracture without flinching, and aims all correction at restoration rather than at expulsion.

That triangle — **standard, grace, restoration** — is the same triangle every eldership, staff team, and ministry board negotiates. The social sciences have studied its mechanics carefully: why people defend behavior they privately doubt (cognitive dissonance), why groups split along status lines (social identity theory), why some teams surface problems early and others hide them until they explode (psychological safety), and why the *manner* of correction predicts its outcome more reliably than its severity (organizational and restorative justice research).

This study puts those two bodies of knowledge in conversation. Scripture sets the norm; social science describes the terrain. The goal is leaders who can hold a line without crushing a person.

### How to use this guide

- **Format.** Six sessions of 75–90 minutes. Designed for an elder board, staff team, or leadership cohort of 4–12 people. It can be stretched to twelve weeks by splitting each session at the "Integration" break.
- **Preparation.** Participants read the assigned 1 Corinthians passage twice before the session — once quickly, once slowly with a pen. Leaders read the exegetical notes and at least one academic source per session.
- **Each session contains:** Big Idea → Text → Historical Context → Exegetical Notes → Academic Lens → Integration → Discussion Questions → Leadership Practice → Closing.
- **Bible text.** Quotations follow the ESV/NIV sense; read from whatever translation your group uses, and where the notes flag a Greek term, compare two translations aloud.

### Four cautions before you begin

1. **1 Corinthians is a pastoral letter, not a policy manual.** It is Paul's contextual response to specific reports (1:11; 5:1; 7:1; 16:17). Extract principles and patterns; resist the urge to lift procedures directly out of the first century.
2. **Social science is descriptive; Scripture is normative.** Festinger tells you *what people do* under dissonance. Paul tells you what the church *is* and what love *requires*. Never let a research finding overrule a command — but do let it explain why obedience is hard.
3. **Managerial language can become a hiding place.** "Psychological safety," "alignment," and "team health" are useful. They are also easy to weaponize as pressure toward silence and conformity. If a framework in this study is being used to protect the institution from the truth, it has been inverted.
4. **Power asymmetry changes everything.** Church discipline processes have a documented history of being turned against the vulnerable. Session 3 addresses this directly and sets non-negotiable red lines. Do not skip that material.

### Group covenant (read aloud in Session 1)

We will speak candidly about our own church. We will not use real names of people outside this room in ways that would embarrass them. We will treat disagreement in this room as a service to the church, not a threat to it. What is said here is not repeated as gossip. We will each name at least one thing we have done wrong before we name what someone else has done wrong.

## Session 1 — Grace First: The Foundation Beneath the Standard

**Texts:** 1 Corinthians 1:1–17; 6:9–11; 15:9–10

### Big Idea

Paul establishes identity before he issues demands. Every imperative in this letter rests on an indicative that has already been given. Leaders who reverse that order produce either despair or defensiveness — and the research on why is remarkably specific.

### Historical Context

Roman Corinth was a refounded colony (44 BC), a commercial hub with a reputation for social mobility, competitive rhetoric, and patron–client networks. Status was publicly performed and constantly contested (Winter, *After Paul Left Corinth*, 2001; Meeks, *The First Urban Christians*, 1983). A congregation drawn from that culture would naturally organize itself around honor, patronage, and the reputational value of one's teacher. Paul's opening subverts the entire economy.

### Exegetical Notes

- **1:2 — "sanctified in Christ Jesus, called to be saints."** The perfect passive is decisive. They *are* holy before they *act* holy. Paul says this to the church he is about to accuse of incest tolerance, litigation, drunkenness at communion, and factionalism.
- **1:4–9 — thanksgiving.** Paul thanks God for the grace, speech, knowledge, and gifts of the very congregation he is about to correct. This is not flattery; it is theological accuracy that also happens to be strategically wise.
- **1:9 — "God is faithful."** The guarantee of the outcome is God's character, not the church's performance. This is the ground of a leader's nerve.
- **6:9–11 — "and such were some of you. But you were washed."** Note the sequence: a demanding moral catalogue, then a past-tense verb of grace, then "washed, sanctified, justified." Paul's highest standards and his most unconditional grace sit in the same paragraph, four verses apart.
- **15:9–10 — "I am the least of the apostles… by the grace of God I am what I am."** Paul models the posture he asks of them: honest about failure, secure in grace, and *therefore* able to work hard rather than hide.

**Theological frame.** John Barclay (*Paul and the Gift*, 2015) argues that Paul's grace is *incongruous* — given without regard to the worth of the recipient — but not *unconditional in its aftermath*. Grace creates obligation precisely because it is a gift, not a wage. This distinction dissolves the false choice most leadership teams live inside: "If we emphasize grace, we lose the standard; if we hold the standard, we lose grace."

### Academic Lens: Dissonance, Self-Concept, and Self-Affirmation

- **Festinger (1957), *A Theory of Cognitive Dissonance*.** Holding two inconsistent cognitions produces aversive arousal, which people reduce by the cheapest available route — usually by changing the cognition that is easiest to change, not the one that is most true.
- **Aronson's self-consistency revision (1968; 1992).** Dissonance is sharpest when new information threatens the self-concept as *competent and moral*. This is why correction of a long-serving volunteer is far more explosive than correction of a newcomer: more self-concept is invested.
- **Steele (1988), self-affirmation theory; Sherman & Cohen (2006).** When a person's global self-worth is affirmed on a dimension they value, they process threatening information *less defensively* and are more likely to accept it. The affirmation must be genuine and must not be about the behavior under review.
- **Cooper & Fazio (1984).** Dissonance is triggered most strongly by feeling personally responsible for an aversive outcome — which is the precise psychological content of conviction.

### Integration

Paul's opening thanksgiving is, functionally, a self-affirmation intervention forty centuries before the experiment — and it is not a technique, because it is true. He affirms them on the dimension they actually value (gifting, knowledge, speech), which frees them to hear an accusation about the dimension they have neglected (love, holiness, unity) without their entire identity collapsing.

The pastoral inference: **a person can absorb a hard truth about their behavior only to the degree that their identity is secured elsewhere.** Grace is not the anaesthetic that makes correction painless. It is the load-bearing wall that lets correction happen without the building coming down.

The failure modes are symmetrical:

| Approach | Mechanism | Predictable Result |
|---|---|---|
| Standard without grace | Identity threat; dissonance resolved by rejecting the source | Defensiveness, exit, or compliance without repentance |
| Grace without standard | No dissonance is generated at all | Drift; the standard becomes decorative |
| Grace as the ground of standard | Identity secure; dissonance directed at behavior | Godly grief and durable change (cf. 2 Cor 7:10) |

### Discussion Questions

1. Read 1:4–9 and 1:10–13 back to back. If you had received this letter, what would the transition have felt like? What does Paul risk by beginning with thanksgiving?
2. Where in your church's culture is the *indicative* actually preached — "you are washed, sanctified, justified" — and where do people functionally believe their standing depends on their performance?
3. Aronson predicts that the most invested people react worst to correction. Test it against your experience. Who in your ministry is most fragile under critique, and why?
4. Is there anyone you have corrected whose identity you never affirmed? What did that cost?
5. Barclay's distinction: grace is unearned but not undemanding. Can you state that in one sentence a new believer would understand?

### Leadership Practice

Each participant identifies one hard conversation they are currently avoiding. Draft only the *first ninety seconds* of it, in writing, following Paul's sequence: (a) something true and specific you are grateful for in this person, unrelated to the problem; (b) the standard, stated as something you both hold; (c) the observed behavior, described without adjectives. Read two of these aloud and critique them.

### Closing

Read 1 Corinthians 1:4–9 aloud as a prayer over your own leadership team, inserting your church's name.

## Session 2 — Factions and Identity: Why Teams Split

**Texts:** 1 Corinthians 1:10–17; 3:1–23; 4:1–7

### Big Idea

The Corinthian divisions were not doctrinal. They were about identity, status, and loyalty to personalities. Paul's remedy is not a compromise between the parties but a relocation of the group's identity to something none of the parties own.

### Historical Context

Margaret Mitchell (*Paul and the Rhetoric of Reconciliation*, 1991) argues that 1:10 is the thesis statement of the entire letter, and that 1 Corinthians follows the conventions of Greco-Roman *deliberative rhetoric* urging **homonoia** ("concord") on a divided city. Ancient concord speeches had standard moves: appeal to a common good, shame over factionalism, and the body-politic analogy. Paul uses all three — then detonates the last one (see Session 5).

### Exegetical Notes

- **1:10 — *schismata*.** A tearing, as of cloth. Paul appeals "by the name of our Lord Jesus Christ" — the only name that outranks the party names.
- **1:12 — "I follow Paul… Apollos… Cephas… Christ."** Note the fourth slogan. The most spiritual-sounding faction is still a faction. Groups that claim to be above the conflict are usually the most damaging participants in it.
- **1:17 — "not with words of eloquent wisdom."** Paul refuses the very skill the Corinthians used to rank their teachers. He declines to compete on the axis that created the split.
- **3:1–4 — *sarkikos* vs *pneumatikos*.** Paul's diagnostic: factionalism is not a leadership problem or a personality problem; it is evidence of immaturity. "Are you not merely human?"
- **3:5–9 — "I planted, Apollos watered, but God gave the growth."** Three moves in one sentence: role differentiation (different jobs), status equalization ("he who plants and he who waters are one"), and attribution of outcome to God. Note 3:8: distinct reward, shared enterprise.
- **3:21–23 — "all things are yours."** Paul inverts the ownership claim. They said "I belong to Paul"; Paul says "Paul belongs to you, and you to Christ."
- **4:1–2 — *oikonomos*, steward.** Accountability runs vertically to the master, not horizontally to the fan base.
- **4:6–7 — "that none of you may be puffed up… What do you have that you did not receive?"** The anti-boasting argument is a grace argument. Comparison dies where gift is acknowledged.

### Academic Lens: Identity, Stages, and Conflict Avoidance

- **Tajfel & Turner (1979), social identity theory.** People derive self-esteem from group membership and therefore favor in-groups and derogate out-groups — *even when the groups are arbitrary*. Minimal difference is sufficient to generate bias. "I follow Apollos" is a textbook social identity claim, and its psychological payoff is self-esteem, not theology.
- **Sherif et al. (1961), Robbers Cave.** Competition between groups produced rapid hostility; contact alone did not fix it. Only **superordinate goals** — objectives requiring genuine interdependence — reduced conflict. Paul's superordinate goal is the cross (1:18–2:5) and the growth God gives (3:6–7).
- **Tuckman (1965); Tuckman & Jensen (1977).** Forming → storming → norming → performing → adjourning. Storming is developmentally normal, not a sign of failure. Teams that suppress storming do not skip it; they defer it, usually at higher cost.
- **Lencioni (2002), *The Five Dysfunctions of a Team*.** Absence of trust produces *fear of conflict*, which produces "artificial harmony," which produces lack of commitment, avoidance of accountability, and inattention to results. Church cultures are unusually vulnerable because niceness is easily mistaken for holiness.
- **Janis (1972), groupthink.** Cohesive groups under pressure suppress dissent, self-censor, and construct an illusion of unanimity. Corinth had the opposite pathology at the congregational level (open factionalism) and may have had groupthink *within* each faction.
- **Hackman (2002), *Leading Teams*.** Effective teams require a real team (bounded, stable, interdependent), a compelling direction, an enabling structure, a supportive context, and competent coaching. Paul's 3:5–9 supplies direction and structure in three sentences.

### Integration

Social identity theory explains why Paul does not adjudicate between Paul-party and Apollos-party. Arbitration would ratify the categories. Instead he dissolves the categories by making all leaders servants (3:5), all outcomes God's (3:6), and all possessions the church's (3:21). This is a superordinate-goal strategy pursued through theological re-description.

Second integration: Paul demonstrates that **conflict is not the enemy of unity — factionalism is.** He is in open, named conflict with the Corinthians throughout the letter, precisely in order to end their factionalism. Lencioni's distinction between productive ideological conflict and destructive interpersonal politics maps onto Paul's distinction between admonition (4:14) and party spirit (1:12).

### Discussion Questions

1. List the informal coalitions in your church without naming individuals. What is each one *for*? What identity does membership supply?
2. Which faction in your context sounds most spiritual? What does 1:12's fourth slogan suggest about it?
3. Paul refuses to compete on eloquence (1:17). What is the axis of competition in your church culture — attendance, theological precision, cultural relevance, longevity? What would it cost you to decline to compete on it?
4. Where does your team practise artificial harmony? Name a specific meeting where the real conversation happened in the parking lot afterwards.
5. What is your genuine superordinate goal — one that cannot be accomplished by any subgroup alone? Is it operative, or only stated?
6. Tuckman predicts storming. When your team last stormed, did leadership treat it as sin or as development?

### Leadership Practice

**Faction map (20 minutes).** On a whiteboard, draw the informal groupings in your congregation as circles, sized by influence rather than number. Draw lines of tension. Then, individually and silently, each leader writes which circle they personally belong to. Compare. The exercise usually reveals that leadership is not above the map.

Then answer in writing: what one interdependent project would force two of those circles to need each other?

### Closing

Read 1 Corinthians 4:6–7 and let each person answer aloud: "One thing I have that I did not earn is ______."

## Session 3 — When Correction Is Required: Discipline and Its Purpose

**Texts:** 1 Corinthians 5:1–13; 6:1–8; with Matthew 18:15–20; Galatians 6:1–5; 2 Corinthians 2:5–11; 7:8–13

> **This is the session where a leadership team most needs to slow down.** Read the safeguarding section before the discussion questions, not after.

### Big Idea

1 Corinthians 5 is the New Testament's most severe disciplinary text — and 2 Corinthians 2 is its sequel, where Paul urges the same church to forgive, comfort, and reaffirm love for the disciplined man. Reading either without the other produces a distortion. Discipline in Paul is a *loop that closes in restoration*, not a mechanism of removal.

### Historical Context

The case in 5:1 is a man in a sexual relationship with his stepmother — a union prohibited by both Torah (Lev 18:8) and Roman law (*incestum*). Paul's shock is that the church was not merely tolerating it but *boasting* (5:2, 6). Bruce Winter and others suggest the offender was likely a person of social standing whose patronage the church valued; Corinthian status dynamics gave the congregation a strong incentive not to act. Fee (*NICNT*, rev. 2014) and Thiselton (*NIGTC*, 2000) both note that the problem Paul addresses is at least as much the **congregation's complacency** as the individual's sin.

### Exegetical Notes

- **5:1–2 — "and you are arrogant!"** The rebuke lands on the church before it lands on the man. Tolerance here is a symptom of the boasting problem from chapters 1–4.
- **5:2, 13 — "remove," *exairō*.** Verse 13 quotes Deuteronomy's purge formula, applied to the community, not the state, and without any physical penalty.
- **5:4–5 — "when you are assembled… hand this man over to Satan for the destruction of the flesh, so that his spirit may be saved."** Three things to notice. It is **corporate** (the assembled church, not a private decision by leaders). It is **public in process**. And its **stated purpose is salvific** — the *hina* clause is remedial, not punitive. Whatever else "hand over to Satan" means, Paul names the goal as rescue.
- **5:6–8 — leaven.** The rationale is contagion and communal integrity, tied to Passover: "Christ, our Passover lamb, has been sacrificed." Even the discipline chapter is grounded in the cross.
- **5:9–11 — scope.** Paul clarifies that he never meant withdrawal from unbelievers. The list in v.11 includes the greedy and the reviler alongside the sexually immoral — categories churches rarely discipline, which is worth sitting with.
- **5:12–13 — "Is it not those inside the church whom you are to judge? God judges those outside."** The church's disciplinary jurisdiction is internal and voluntary. Combined with 4:5 ("do not pronounce judgment before the Lord comes… who will disclose the purposes of the heart"), Paul both authorizes and strictly limits ecclesial judgment: judge public behavior, not hidden motive.
- **6:1–8 — lawsuits.** Two failures: the church cannot resolve internal disputes, and members are willing to shame one another publicly for gain. **6:7 — "Why not rather suffer wrong?"** Paul is prepared to say the process of vindication can itself be sinful. Note carefully: this is addressed to *property and honor disputes between peers*, and has been catastrophically misapplied to abuse and crime.
- **2 Corinthians 2:5–11 — the loop closes.** "The punishment by the majority is enough… you should forgive and comfort him… reaffirm your love for him… so that we would not be outwitted by Satan." Paul who invoked Satan in discipline now invokes Satan in the *refusal to restore*. Both excess and deficiency of severity are demonic.
- **2 Corinthians 7:8–13 — *lupē kata theon*, "grief according to God."** Godly grief produces repentance and leads to life; worldly grief produces death. Paul is explicit that he caused pain, that he does not regret it, and that the *fruit* is the criterion by which the pain is judged.
- **Galatians 6:1 — "you who are spiritual… restore him in a spirit of gentleness, keeping watch on yourself, lest you too be tempted."** The corrector's self-awareness is part of the process.
- **Matthew 18:15–17 — escalation.** Private → witnesses → church. Note the direction: correction begins at the lowest level of publicity and escalates only as necessary. Corinth skipped straight past every stage by doing nothing.

### Academic Lens: Justice, Shame, and the Watching Room

**Organizational justice — why the *how* determines the outcome.**
- **Adams (1965), equity theory.** People assess fairness by comparing their own outcome/input ratio to others'. Selective enforcement — disciplining the powerless while excusing the patron — is perceived as injustice even when the ruling itself is correct. This is precisely Corinth's failure.
- **Thibaut & Walker (1975); Leventhal (1980).** *Procedural* justice — consistency, voice, impartiality, correctability — drives acceptance of unfavourable outcomes more strongly than the outcomes themselves.
- **Bies & Moag (1986); Colquitt (2001).** Four dimensions to audit in any disciplinary process: **distributive** (is the outcome proportionate?), **procedural** (was the process consistent and voiced?), **interpersonal** (was the person treated with dignity?), and **informational** (were the reasons explained truthfully?). A process can be right on one and fail on three.
- **Treviño (1992), "The social effects of punishment in organizations."** Discipline communicates to *observers*, not only to the disciplined. Third parties draw conclusions about what the community actually values. Under-punishment of a favoured member teaches everyone that the standard is negotiable; harsh or arbitrary punishment teaches everyone to hide.
- **Schein (2017), *Organizational Culture and Leadership*.** Culture is embedded most powerfully by what leaders pay attention to and how they react to critical incidents. Your handling of one hard case will shape your church's real (as opposed to stated) values more than a year of sermons on holiness.

**Shame, guilt, and reintegration.**
- **Braithwaite (1989), *Crime, Shame and Reintegration*.** The decisive variable is not the presence of shame but its type. **Stigmatizing shame** attaches to the person, severs the bond, and pushes the offender toward deviant subcultures. **Reintegrative shame** attaches to the act, is expressed within a maintained relationship, and is followed by gestures of reacceptance. Braithwaite's condition — disapproval of the act *within* a continuing bond of respect — is an almost exact description of 1 Corinthians 5 read together with 2 Corinthians 2.
- **Zehr (2002), *The Little Book of Restorative Justice*.** Reframes the central questions from "what rule was broken and what does the offender deserve?" to "who was harmed, what are their needs, and whose obligation is it to repair?" Note what this adds to 1 Corinthians 5, where the victim is largely invisible in the text: a restorative frame insists that the harmed party's needs are part of the process, not a footnote to it.
- **Brown (2012), *Daring Greatly*.** Guilt ("I did something bad") is adaptive and correlates with repair behaviour; shame ("I am bad") correlates with defensiveness, addiction, and aggression. This is the empirical shadow of Paul's godly/worldly grief distinction.
- **Dissonance connection (Aronson, 1992; Steele, 1988).** When correction is framed as an identity verdict, dissonance is resolved by defending the self: denial, minimization, counter-attack, or exit. When correction is framed as a behavioural gap *while identity is affirmed*, dissonance can be resolved by changing the behaviour. **The framing of the rebuke largely determines which route the person takes.**

### Integration

Put the two literatures side by side and a coherent doctrine of correction emerges:

| Element | 1 Corinthians / 2 Corinthians | Social science equivalent |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose is rescue (1 Cor 5:5) | Remedial *hina* clause | Restorative rather than retributive justice (Zehr) |
| Corporate, assembled, public process (5:4) | Not a private leadership decision | Procedural justice: transparency, correctability |
| Consistency across status (5:11 list; 4:6) | Greedy and reviler listed alongside | Equity theory; selective enforcement destroys legitimacy |
| Judge behaviour, not motive (4:5) | Eschatological reserve | Act-focused not person-focused disapproval |
| Gentleness and self-suspicion (Gal 6:1) | "lest you too be tempted" | Interpersonal justice; corrector's humility |
| Reaffirm love; forgive; comfort (2 Cor 2:7–8) | Explicit gestures of reacceptance | Reintegrative shaming's decisive final step |
| Grief that leads to life (2 Cor 7:10) | Godly vs worldly grief | Guilt vs shame; productive vs defensive dissonance |
| Observers learn the culture (5:6 leaven) | Contagion logic | Treviño's third-party effects; Schein's critical incidents |

**The single most common failure in church discipline is not excessive severity. It is an unclosed loop** — a process with a mechanism for exclusion and no mechanism for restoration. Braithwaite's research predicts exactly what churches then observe: people who never come back, and a congregation that learns to hide.

### Non-negotiable red lines: abuse, crime, and power

The disciplinary framework above assumes rough parity between parties and a matter internal to the church. Several categories fall outside it entirely, and treating them as internal discipline compounds the original harm:

1. **Criminal conduct — especially any sexual offence, any offence against a child, and any physical violence — is reported to civil authorities.** Romans 13:1–7 assigns the sword to the state, not the elders. Internal process never substitutes for, delays, or conditions reporting.
2. **1 Corinthians 6:7 ("why not rather suffer wrong?") is not applicable to abuse or crime.** It addresses peers litigating over property and honour. Using it to discourage a victim from seeking legal protection is a misuse of Scripture with a documented history of harm.
3. **The victim is never subject to the same process as the offender**, and is never asked to participate in reconciliation as a condition of the church's care.
4. **Confidentiality protects process integrity, never institutional reputation.** If a policy would silence a harmed person to protect the church's name, it is not a discipline policy.
5. **Where an accusation involves a leader, that leader has no role in the process** — no voice in scope, no access to the file, no contact with the complainant. Independent, external investigation is the norm, not the exception.
6. **Forgiveness and consequences are distinct.** Forgiveness is commanded (2 Cor 2:7). Restoration to a position of trust or authority is not. The absence of ongoing consequence is not evidence of grace.

### Discussion Questions

1. Read 1 Corinthians 5 and 2 Corinthians 2:5–11 aloud in sequence. What does it do to your reading of chapter 5 to know how the story ends?
2. Paul's rebuke in 5:2 targets the church's arrogance before the man's sin. Where is your congregation's tolerance of something a symptom of what it boasts about?
3. Verse 11 names the greedy and the reviler. Why are these almost never disciplined? What does that inconsistency teach your congregation about your real standards (Adams; Treviño)?
4. Score your most recent hard case on Colquitt's four dimensions — distributive, procedural, interpersonal, informational. Which did you fail?
5. Braithwaite: did your last corrective conversation attach disapproval to the act or to the person? What specifically did you say that makes you sure?
6. Does your church have a written, rehearsed pathway *back*? Who is responsible for initiating restoration, and by when?
7. Read the red lines section. Where is your current practice or policy inadequate, and who will own fixing it this quarter?

### Leadership Practice

**Draft your pathway (35 minutes; see Appendix A).** In pairs, draft one stage each of a written correction-and-restoration pathway: (1) informal private conversation, (2) documented conversation with a second leader, (3) formal process with defined scope and voice for the accused, (4) congregational or membership action, (5) **restoration pathway with named owner and review dates**, (6) safeguarding escalation that bypasses stages 1–4 entirely for criminal matters.

Then stress-test the whole draft against a single question: *if the person at the centre of this were the largest donor, would the pathway function identically?*

### Closing

Read 2 Corinthians 2:7–8 slowly, twice. Sit in silence for a minute. Then pray for one person your church has lost through a loop that never closed.

## Session 4 — Disputable Matters: Standards Without Legalism

**Texts:** 1 Corinthians 8:1–13; 9:19–23; 10:23–33; with 11:1

### Big Idea

Not every standard carries the same weight. Paul distinguishes between what is essential, what is communally binding, and what belongs to conscience — and he insists that knowledge which is *correct* can still be *destructive* when it is not governed by love. This is where most church conflict actually lives: not over the gospel, but over disputable matters treated as gospel.

### Historical Context

Meat sold in Corinthian markets and served at civic banquets was frequently connected to temple sacrifice. Participation was bound up with social obligation, guild membership, patronage, and civic identity. To abstain was to accept economic and social cost; to participate was, for some believers, indistinguishable from idolatry. Winter (2001) and Garland (*BECNT*, 2003) both stress that this was a live question of social survival, not an abstract dietary scruple. The "strong" were likely the higher-status members with the most to lose by withdrawing.

### Exegetical Notes

- **8:1 — "knowledge puffs up, but love builds up."** *Oikodomeō* — builds up — becomes the letter's criterion for evaluating any practice (cf. 10:23; 14:3, 12, 26).
- **8:4–6 — Paul concedes the theology.** "An idol has no real existence… there is one God." The strong are *right*. Paul does not correct their doctrine; he corrects the use they make of it.
- **8:7–12 — *syneidēsis*, conscience.** Paul takes a weak conscience seriously as a pastoral reality even while regarding it as insufficiently informed. Wounding it is not a minor cost: "you sin against Christ" (8:12).
- **8:9 — "take care that this right of yours does not become a stumbling block."** Rights are real and voluntarily limited. This is the whole argument of chapter 9 in miniature.
- **9:1–18 — Paul's own rights renounced.** He has the right to support, to marry, to be paid; he declines. He argues at length for a right in order to demonstrate the freedom not to use it.
- **9:19–23 — "I have become all things to all people."** Radical flexibility in method, bounded by an unbending goal ("that I might save some") and by "under the law of Christ" (9:21). Flexibility is not relativism because the telos is fixed.
- **9:24–27 — "I discipline my body."** Self-discipline precedes the disciplining of others. Note the order in the letter: Paul's self-restraint (ch. 9) comes before his instruction on communal order (ch. 11–14).
- **10:1–13 — Israel as warning.** Freedom has a limit: idolatry. "Therefore let anyone who thinks that he stands take heed lest he fall" (10:12). Confidence is the risk factor.
- **10:23–24 — "All things are lawful, but not all things are helpful… let no one seek his own good, but the good of his neighbour."** The decision rule is not permission but edification.
- **10:31–11:1 — "whether you eat or drink… do all to the glory of God… Be imitators of me, as I am of Christ."** The chain of imitation runs through the cross, which is why self-limitation is the pattern.

**A working triage.** Albert Mohler's popular framework of "theological triage" (2004) offers a usable heuristic that is broadly consistent with Paul's practice here: first-order matters are essential to the gospel; second-order matters legitimately define congregational or denominational practice; third-order matters are held with charity and do not determine fellowship. Paul's meat question sits in the third tier — while the idolatry question in chapter 10 sits in the first. **Much church conflict is a misclassification error.**

### Academic Lens: Dissonance, Espoused Theory, and Hypocrisy Induction

- **Festinger & Carlsmith (1959).** Participants paid $1 to say a boring task was interesting changed their private attitude to match; those paid $20 did not. Insufficient external justification for a behaviour drives internal attitude change. Implication for churches: **behaviour that people adopt under heavy external pressure produces less internalized conviction than behaviour they adopt with weak external pressure.** Coerced compliance is psychologically shallow.
- **Aronson & Mills (1959), effort justification.** Participants who underwent a severe initiation rated the group as more attractive. People defend most fiercely the positions they have paid the most for. This explains why long-serving members defend inherited standards with an intensity out of proportion to the standard's importance — and why a leader who dismisses a third-tier tradition is heard as attacking a lifetime of sacrifice.
- **Argyris & Schön (1974); Argyris (1990).** The gap between **espoused theory** (what we say we believe) and **theory-in-use** (what our behaviour reveals) is the central object of organizational learning. *Defensive routines* protect people from the embarrassment of seeing that gap, and are self-sealing: they are undiscussable, and their undiscussability is itself undiscussable. **Single-loop learning** adjusts actions within existing assumptions; **double-loop learning** questions the assumptions. Paul is consistently forcing double-loop learning on Corinth: he does not tell them how to run the Supper better, he asks what they think the church *is* (11:22).
- **Stone, Aronson, Crain, Winslow & Fried (1994), hypocrisy induction.** Making people publicly advocate a position, then reminding them of their own inconsistent behaviour, produced significantly greater behaviour change than information alone. The dissonance between stated value and actual practice is a *lever*, not merely a problem.
- **Dweck (2006), mindset.** Fixed-mindset framing ("this is who you are") suppresses effort after failure; growth framing ("this is what you did and can change") preserves it. Directly relevant to how a standard is communicated to someone who has failed it.

### Integration

Paul uses hypocrisy induction repeatedly and deliberately. "You are puffed up" while tolerating incest (5:2). "Why not rather suffer wrong?" while suing each other (6:7). "It is not the Lord's supper that you eat" while calling it that (11:20). In each case he holds up the community's own stated identity against its behaviour and lets the dissonance do the work. **Note the crucial difference from shaming: the standard he invokes is one they already claim, so the dissonance points toward integrity rather than toward a verdict on their worth.**

Equally important is the *reverse* application. On disputable matters, the strong are theologically correct and pastorally wrong. Argyris would call this a single-loop response: they have optimized their behaviour within the assumption that being right is the objective. Paul's double-loop question is: *what is the objective?* Answer: building up (8:1), the good of the neighbour (10:24), the glory of God (10:31).

Practical upshot for standards-setting:
- **Tier your standards explicitly and in writing.** Undocumented standards are enforced inconsistently, which is a justice failure (Session 3).
- **Expect effort justification.** When you propose changing a second- or third-tier practice, the resistance is proportional to what people have invested, not to the practice's importance. Name the investment before you name the change.
- **Prefer minimal sufficient pressure.** Festinger & Carlsmith predicts that heavy coercion yields compliance without conviction — the pattern churches later describe as "they left as soon as they could."
- **Audit espoused vs actual.** The gap is where your real culture lives.

### Discussion Questions

1. Name three standards your church holds. Sort them into first-, second-, and third-tier. Where do you disagree with each other — and what does that disagreement predict about future conflict?
2. Paul concedes the strong are right and still tells them to stop (8:4–13). When did you last decline to exercise a freedom you were entitled to, for someone else's sake? What did it cost?
3. Where in your church is a third-tier preference being enforced as though it were first-tier? Who is paying for that?
4. Espoused vs theory-in-use: complete the sentence honestly for your church — "We say we value ______, but our calendar, budget, and staffing reveal that we actually value ______."
5. Argyris says defensive routines are undiscussable, and their undiscussability is undiscussable. What is currently undiscussable on your team? Say it now.
6. If Festinger & Carlsmith are right that heavy external pressure produces shallow internalization, what does that imply about how your church secures compliance with its standards?

### Leadership Practice

**Standards audit (see Appendix C).** Build a three-column table: *Standard* | *Tier (1/2/3)* | *Written down? Enforced consistently? Last reviewed?* Populate it with at least twelve entries drawn from your actual practice — including unwritten ones about dress, language, alcohol, politics, giving, and attendance. Circle every row where the tier and the enforcement intensity do not match.

### Closing

Read 1 Corinthians 8:1–3 and 10:31–33 together. Ask each person to name one right they will lay down this month.

## Session 5 — One Body, Many Members: Status, Gifts, and Psychological Safety

**Texts:** 1 Corinthians 11:17–34; 12:1–31; 13:1–13; 14:26–40

### Big Idea

Paul's answer to a stratified, competitive congregation is a body in which the *less honourable* members receive *greater* honour, love is the criterion for every gift, and everything is measured by whether it builds up. This is the theological foundation of what organizational research now calls psychological safety — and it is far more radical than the research.

### Historical Context

The Lord's Supper in Corinth was held in a domestic setting, probably a wealthier member's house. Gerd Theissen (*The Social Setting of Pauline Christianity*, 1982) and Wayne Meeks argue the abuse in 11:21 reflects standard Greco-Roman dinner conventions, where guests of higher status received better food, more wine, and earlier seating in the *triclinium* while others ate in the *atrium* and arrived later because their work did not stop. The scandal Paul names is not gluttony in the abstract; it is **social stratification enacted at the table of a crucified Lord.**

On chapter 12: the body-politic analogy was a stock rhetorical device for urging the lower classes to accept their place. Livy (2.32) records Menenius Agrippa deploying it against a plebeian secession — the belly and limbs need each other, so stop complaining. Every educated Corinthian would have recognized the topos. **Paul takes the establishment's tool for enforcing hierarchy and inverts it** (12:22–24): the parts seeming weaker are indispensable, and the less honourable receive greater honour. Dale Martin (*The Corinthian Body*, 1995) and Mitchell (1991) both regard this inversion as the rhetorical hinge of the letter.

### Exegetical Notes

- **11:17–22 — "when you come together it is not for the better but for the worse… you humiliate those who have nothing."** The gathering itself has become the site of harm. "Do you despise the church of God?"
- **11:20 — "it is not the Lord's supper that you eat."** The name no longer fits the practice. A precise espoused-theory/theory-in-use indictment.
- **11:27–29 — *dokimazō* (examine) and *diakrinōn to sōma* (discerning the body).** Given the context, "the body" carries the congregational sense: failing to recognize the church-as-body is what makes the eating unworthy. Self-examination, note, is prescribed for *everyone* — the standard applies first to the self.
- **11:30–32 — "when we are judged by the Lord, we are disciplined so that we may not be condemned."** Divine discipline is explicitly framed as preservative, not condemnatory. The theology of Session 3 in one verse.
- **12:4–7 — varieties of gifts, same Spirit, "for the common good."** Diversity is by design, and its purpose is corporate.
- **12:11 — "apportions to each one individually as he wills."** Gift distribution is sovereign, which removes both boasting (12:21) and self-deprecation (12:15–16) as legitimate options.
- **12:15–20 — the foot and the ear.** Paul addresses *self*-exclusion first — the person who disqualifies themselves — before he addresses the one who excludes others. Most church leaders reverse this priority.
- **12:22–26 — "the parts that we think less honourable we clothe with greater honour… that there may be no division in the body… If one member suffers, all suffer together."** The redistribution of honour is deliberate and countercultural. And the metric of unity is *shared suffering and shared rejoicing*, not agreement.
- **13:1–3 — gifts without love are nothing.** Placed *between* the two chapters on gifts, chapter 13 functions as the criterion, not an interlude. Applied to leadership: correction without love is noise.
- **13:4–7 — patient, kind, not arrogant or rude, not irritable or resentful, bears all things.** Read this as a job description for the person conducting a disciplinary conversation.
- **14:3, 12, 26 — "let all things be done for building up."** The functional test for every practice.
- **14:29–33 — "let the others weigh what is said… the spirits of prophets are subject to prophets… God is not a God of confusion but of peace."** Structured mutual evaluation of contributions. This is a governance principle: even Spirit-inspired speech is subject to communal weighing.
- **14:40 — "all things should be done decently and in order."** Order serves edification; it is not an end in itself. Note that this order-verse concludes a chapter that *presumes* broad participation (14:26, "each one has").

### Academic Lens: Psychological Safety and Team Composition

- **Edmondson (1999), "Psychological safety and learning behavior in work teams," *Administrative Science Quarterly*.** Psychological safety is the shared belief that the team is safe for interpersonal risk-taking — that speaking up will not lead to humiliation or punishment. In her original hospital study, better-led nursing units reported *more* errors, not fewer: safety increased *detection and reporting*, which is what enables improvement. **The implication for churches is uncomfortable: a congregation with no reported problems may be less healthy than one with many.**
- **Edmondson (2018), *The Fearless Organization*.** Safety is not comfort, niceness, or lowered standards. Her two-by-two: high standards + low safety = anxiety; low standards + high safety = complacency; low + low = apathy; **high standards + high safety = learning and performance.** This grid is arguably the cleanest secular restatement of grace-and-standards in the literature, and worth drawing on a whiteboard.
- **Rozovsky (2015), Google's Project Aristotle.** Across 180 teams, *who* was on the team predicted performance poorly; how the team interacted predicted it well. Psychological safety was the strongest of five factors (with dependability, structure and clarity, meaning, and impact).
- **Katzenbach & Smith (1993), *The Wisdom of Teams*.** A working group is not a team. Real teams have shared work products, mutual accountability, and complementary skills. Many church "teams" are working groups with a shared meeting.
- **Belbin (1981), *Management Teams*.** Effective teams need a distribution of behavioural roles, and every strength carries an "allowable weakness." Useful as a secular parallel to 1 Corinthians 12 — with an important theological caveat: Belbin's roles are traits to be managed; Paul's *charismata* are sovereign gifts to be honoured.
- **Bandura (1997), collective efficacy.** A group's shared belief in its capacity to accomplish its task predicts its persistence under difficulty, over and above individual ability. Compare 1 Corinthians 15:58.
- **Bonhoeffer (1954), *Life Together*.** "He who loves his dream of a community more than the Christian community itself becomes a destroyer of the latter." Bonhoeffer's "wish dream" is the theological name for what Lencioni calls artificial harmony: the idealized community that must be protected from the real one. His chapter on the ministry of holding one's tongue and the ministry of listening belongs alongside Edmondson.

### Integration

Edmondson's high-standards/high-safety quadrant is where this entire study has been heading, and 1 Corinthians supplies both axes with content the research cannot:

- **The standard** is not organizational performance but the cross and the character of Christ (11:26; 13:4–7).
- **The safety** is not a management practice but the honour redistribution of 12:22–26 — actively clothing the less honourable with greater honour.

Two integrations deserve special attention.

First, **11:17–34 is a case study in what happens when a team's real hierarchy overrides its stated theology.** Nobody in Corinth voted to humiliate the poor. The stratification was simply the default cultural script, executed without deliberation. Schein's insight applies: culture operates at the level of unexamined assumption. Paul's intervention is to make the script visible and name what it means — "it is not the Lord's supper that you eat."

Second, **14:29 ("let the others weigh what is said") establishes structured dissent as a spiritual practice, not a concession to human weakness.** Janis's groupthink research and Lencioni's fear-of-conflict both prescribe institutionalized challenge. Paul got there by a different route: if the Spirit distributes gifts across the whole body, no single member has access to the whole, and mutual weighing is therefore not distrust but epistemic humility.

### Discussion Questions

1. Read 11:20–22 and ask: what is the equivalent stratification in our gatherings? Who arrives late because their work does not stop? Who eats first?
2. The body-politic topos was normally used to keep the lower orders in place. Paul inverts it. Where does your church use unity language to suppress a legitimate complaint?
3. Draw Edmondson's two-by-two and place your team on it with an honest dot. Then place your congregation. Explain your placement with evidence, not impressions.
4. Edmondson's nurses reported *more* errors on better-led units. How many mistakes were surfaced to your leadership in the last six months? Is that number good news or bad news?
5. 12:15–16 addresses self-exclusion first. Who in your church has quietly disqualified themselves? What would 12:22–24 have you actually do — not feel — about it?
6. Read 13:4–7 as the job description for a disciplinary conversation. Which clause do you personally violate most often?
7. Is your leadership a real team or a working group (Katzenbach & Smith)? What is your shared work product?
8. Does anything in your governance perform the function of 14:29 — a structured mechanism by which "the others weigh what is said"? If not, design one before you leave.

### Leadership Practice

**Two exercises.**

*(a) Safety diagnostic (Appendix D).* Each person scores seven statements 1–5 anonymously — e.g. "If I make a mistake on this team, it is held against me"; "It is safe to take a risk on this team"; "Members of this team are able to bring up problems and tough issues." Collect, average, and — critically — look at the *lowest individual score*, not the mean. The mean tells you how it feels for most; the minimum tells you whether someone is unsafe.

*(b) Honour redistribution.* Identify by name three people in your church whose contribution is genuinely load-bearing and publicly invisible. Decide on one specific, public, non-tokenistic act of honour for each — with a name and a date attached. Then ask why it had not already happened.

### Closing

Read 1 Corinthians 12:24–26 aloud. Name one member of the body who is currently suffering, and pray as those who suffer with them.

## Session 6 — Godly Grief and Resurrection Nerve: Closing the Loop

**Texts:** 2 Corinthians 2:5–11; 7:8–13; 1 Corinthians 15:1–11, 51–58; 16:13–14

### Big Idea

Correction is only finished when restoration has happened and love has been publicly reaffirmed. Leaders can bear the cost of doing this properly — the anxiety, the sabotage, the slowness — because the resurrection guarantees that the labour is not wasted.

### Historical Context

Between 1 and 2 Corinthians lie a painful visit, a severe letter now lost, and a partial reconciliation brokered through Titus (2 Cor 2:1–4, 12–13; 7:5–16). 2 Corinthians shows us the *aftermath* of the discipline urged in 1 Corinthians 5 — which makes it indispensable, and which is why so much church discipline theology is distorted by stopping at the first letter.

### Exegetical Notes

- **2 Cor 2:5–6 — "the punishment by the majority is enough."** Paul sets a terminus. Discipline has a defined endpoint, declared by the community. Indefinite consequence is not discipline; it is exile by attrition.
- **2 Cor 2:7 — "you should rather turn to forgive and comfort him, so that he may not be overwhelmed by excessive sorrow."** The pastoral concern has fully reversed direction. The risk now is that the man is crushed.
- **2 Cor 2:8 — "reaffirm your love for him."** A public, corporate, deliberate act. Braithwaite's "ceremony of reacceptance," commanded.
- **2 Cor 2:10–11 — "so that we would not be outwitted by Satan."** The withheld forgiveness is the satanic opportunity. Compare 1 Cor 5:5, where discipline itself was the handing over to Satan. **Both over-severity and under-restoration serve the same enemy.**
- **2 Cor 7:8–9 — "I do not regret it… you were grieved into repenting."** Paul owns having caused pain and declines to apologize for it, while distinguishing the pain from the purpose.
- **2 Cor 7:10 — "godly grief produces a repentance that leads to salvation without regret, whereas worldly grief produces death."** The criterion is fruit. Not intensity of feeling, not duration of remorse — *fruit*.
- **2 Cor 7:11 — "at every point you have proved yourselves innocent."** Repentance is evidenced, not merely asserted.
- **1 Cor 15:3–8 — the creed.** Underneath the whole letter is a fixed deposit: died, buried, raised, appeared. Standards, discipline, unity, and gifts are all downstream of this.
- **1 Cor 15:9–10 — "by the grace of God I am what I am, and his grace toward me was not in vain. On the contrary, I worked harder."** Grace and effort in a single breath. This is the sentence to memorize against both legalism and passivity.
- **1 Cor 15:58 — "your labour in the Lord is not in vain."** Placed immediately after the resurrection argument. The reason leaders can absorb the cost of doing correction well is eschatological, not managerial.
- **1 Cor 16:13–14 — "Be watchful, stand firm in the faith, act like men, be strong. Let all that you do be done in love."** Four imperatives of nerve, one of love, in the same sentence. The whole study in miniature.

### Academic Lens: Differentiation, Sabotage, and Forgiveness

- **Friedman (2007), *A Failure of Nerve*.** Applying Bowen family systems theory to leadership, Friedman argues that anxious systems pressure leaders toward togetherness at the cost of clarity. The healthy leader is a **self-differentiated, non-anxious presence**: someone who can stay connected to people while remaining clear about their own principles. Crucially, Friedman predicts **sabotage**: when a leader begins to differentiate, the system pushes back hardest *precisely when the change is working*, and the leader's ability to absorb that reaction — without cutting off and without capitulating — is the actual test.
- **Steinke (1993/2006), *How Your Church Family Works*.** Anxiety in congregations is contagious and travels through triangulation: two parties recruit a third rather than address each other. Church leaders are the most triangulated people in the system. The discipline of refusing to carry other people's conversations is directly relevant to 1 Corinthians 6:1–8.
- **Argyris (1990).** Restoration processes fail through defensive routines: everyone knows a person was never truly reintegrated, and no one says so.
- **Volf (1996), *Exclusion and Embrace*; (2005), *Free of Charge*.** Forgiveness involves both naming the wrong truthfully and releasing the claim. Volf's argument that memory must be *redeemed* rather than erased is important against the sentimental version of restoration that requires the harmed party to pretend.
- **Dissonance, one last time.** 2 Corinthians 7:10 distinguishes two responses to the same painful information: one produces repentance and life, the other death. The dissonance literature describes the identical fork — dissonance can be resolved by changing the behaviour (godly grief) or by defending the self through denial, minimization, blame-shifting, source-derogation, or exit (worldly grief). **A leader cannot determine which route a person takes, but the framing of the correction, the security of the person's identity in grace, and the visible availability of a way back all shift the probabilities.**

### Integration

The full arc:

1. **Grace establishes identity** (1 Cor 1:4–9; 6:11) → identity security enables non-defensive processing (Steele; Aronson).
2. **Unity is relocated, not negotiated** (1 Cor 3:5–9) → superordinate goals dissolve in-group competition (Sherif; Tajfel & Turner).
3. **Correction is corporate, consistent, dignified, and remedial** (1 Cor 5) → procedural and interpersonal justice; reintegrative rather than stigmatizing shame (Colquitt; Braithwaite).
4. **Standards are tiered and love-governed** (1 Cor 8–10) → espoused theory aligned with theory-in-use; double-loop learning (Argyris).
5. **Honour flows to the overlooked; dissent is structured** (1 Cor 11–14) → high standards with high psychological safety (Edmondson).
6. **The loop closes in public reaffirmation of love** (2 Cor 2:7–8) → ceremony of reacceptance; grief that leads to life.
7. **The whole thing is underwritten by the resurrection** (1 Cor 15:58) → the source of nerve that no framework can supply.

### Discussion Questions

1. Who has your church disciplined, formally or informally, in the last five years? For each, has the loop closed? Name the person responsible for closing it.
2. "The punishment by the majority is enough" (2 Cor 2:6). Do your processes have a declared endpoint, or do consequences simply persist until forgotten?
3. Paul does not regret causing pain (7:8). Where have you apologized for causing necessary pain, and thereby undermined the correction?
4. Godly grief is identified by fruit (7:11), not by the intensity of an apology. How do you currently distinguish the two? What have you got wrong before?
5. Friedman predicts sabotage at the moment change is working. Where have you interpreted resistance as evidence that you were wrong, when it may have been evidence that you were effective? And — the harder question — how will you tell the difference honestly?
6. Where are you being triangulated right now? What conversation are you carrying that belongs to two other people?
7. Read 1 Corinthians 15:58. What specific piece of leadership labour feels most futile to you at the moment?

### Leadership Practice

**Ninety-day commitments.** Each participant writes three commitments with dates and one named accountability partner from the group:
- one conversation to initiate (Session 1's ninety-second draft, now delivered);
- one structural item to build or fix — pathway, safety mechanism, standards document, or safeguarding gap (Sessions 3–5);
- one act of restoration or honour to complete (Sessions 5–6).

Schedule a single follow-up meeting for day 90 before the group disperses. Put it in calendars now. **A study that does not produce a dated commitment produces only dissonance reduction through discussion** — which is, of itself, one of Argyris's defensive routines.

### Closing

Read 1 Corinthians 16:13–14, then 15:58. Commission one another by name.

# Appendices

## Appendix A — Correction and Restoration Pathway (template)

Adapt to your polity; nothing here overrides your jurisdiction's legal requirements.

**Stage 0 — Safeguarding triage (always first).** Does the matter involve a crime, a minor, a vulnerable adult, sexual misconduct, violence, or a significant power differential? If yes → report to civil authorities and follow the safeguarding policy. Stages 1–4 do not apply, are not a substitute, and must not delay reporting.

**Stage 1 — Private conversation** (Matt 18:15; Gal 6:1). Purpose: clarify facts and invite repentance. Posture: gentleness, self-suspicion. Record: date only.

**Stage 2 — Second conversation with a witness** (Matt 18:16). Purpose: confirm the standard, hear the response, agree specific changes and a review date. Record: written summary shared with the person.

**Stage 3 — Formal process.** Written statement of the concern; the person receives it in advance, has voice, may bring a support person, and may respond in writing. Defined scope, defined decision-makers, defined timeline, defined appeal route. Conflicts of interest declared and recused.

**Stage 4 — Congregational or membership action** (1 Cor 5:4). Corporate, assembled, with the purpose stated aloud as restoration (5:5). What is disclosed is the minimum necessary.

**Stage 5 — Restoration pathway.** *Mandatory, drafted at the same time as Stage 3 or 4, never later.* Named owner; agreed markers of repentance evidenced by fruit (2 Cor 7:11); review dates; the specific public act of reaffirmed love (2 Cor 2:8); and a clear statement of what is restored and what is not (fellowship vs. position of trust).

**Stage 6 — Review of the process itself.** Score against Colquitt's four dimensions. What did we get wrong? Who was harmed by our handling? Record and change the pathway accordingly.

## Appendix B — Correction Conversation Scaffold

1. **Secure the ground.** One specific, true, unrelated affirmation (1 Cor 1:4–9; Steele).
2. **Name the shared standard.** "We both believe…" — invoke a value they already claim (Stone et al.).
3. **Describe the behaviour.** Observable, specific, dated, adjective-free. The act, not the person (Braithwaite; Brown).
4. **Ask, then stop talking.** "What am I missing?" Give voice before verdict (procedural justice).
5. **State the consequence, if any.** Proportionate, consistent with what others have received, explained truthfully (distributive and informational justice).
6. **Define the way back.** Concretely, with a date and an owner. Never leave this conversation without it.
7. **Reaffirm the relationship.** Explicitly, in words, before you leave the room (2 Cor 2:8).
8. **Afterwards, examine yourself.** "Keeping watch on yourself, lest you too be tempted" (Gal 6:1).

**Do not:** use "always"/"never"; recruit a third party first (triangulation); soften the standard to reduce your own discomfort; deliver it by email; or apologize for the standard itself.

Populate with at least twelve rows, including unwritten expectations. Flag every mismatch between tier and enforcement intensity — those are your future conflicts.

## Appendix D — Psychological Safety Inventory (adapted from Edmondson, 1999)

Score 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Items 1 and 4 are reverse-scored. Collect anonymously.

1. If I make a mistake on this team, it is held against me. *(R)*
2. Members of this team are able to bring up problems and tough issues.
3. People on this team never reject others for being different. 
4. It is difficult to ask other members of this team for help. *(R)*
5. It is safe to take a risk on this team.
6. No one on this team would deliberately act in a way that undermines my efforts.
7. My unique skills and talents are valued and utilized.

**Read the minimum, not the mean.** Then ask the theological question 12:22–26 raises: whose score would we expect to be lowest, and have we asked them?

## Appendix F — Glossary

- **Adiaphora** — "indifferent things"; matters neither commanded nor forbidden, left to conscience. Paul's category in 1 Cor 8–10.
- **Charisma / charismata** — grace-gift(s); sovereignly apportioned (1 Cor 12:11), given for the common good (12:7).
- **Cognitive dissonance** — aversive tension from inconsistent cognitions, reduced by the cheapest available route.
- **Defensive routine** — a self-sealing organizational practice that protects people from the embarrassment of seeing the gap between what they say and what they do.
- **Distributive / procedural / interpersonal / informational justice** — the four dimensions on which any disciplinary act is evaluated by participants and observers.
- **Double-loop learning** — questioning the governing assumptions rather than only adjusting actions within them.
- **Espoused theory vs. theory-in-use** — stated values vs. values revealed by behaviour.
- **Homonoia** — "concord"; the goal of Greco-Roman deliberative speeches to divided cities, and the rhetorical genre of 1 Corinthians on Mitchell's reading.
- **Incongruous grace** — grace given without regard to the worth of the recipient (Barclay); unearned yet generative of obligation.
- **Indicative and imperative** — the Pauline order in which what God has done grounds what believers must do.
- **Oikodomē** — building up; Paul's functional test for every practice (1 Cor 8:1; 14:26).
- **Psychological safety** — shared belief that interpersonal risk-taking is safe; a condition for surfacing problems, not a synonym for comfort.
- **Reintegrative vs. stigmatizing shame** — disapproval of the act within a maintained bond, closed by a ceremony of reacceptance, vs. disapproval of the person that severs the bond.
- **Self-affirmation** — securing global self-worth on a valued dimension, which reduces defensive processing of threatening information.
- **Self-differentiation / non-anxious presence** — remaining connected to a system while staying clear about one's own principles; expect sabotage when it starts working.
- **Superordinate goal** — an objective requiring genuine interdependence between rival subgroups; the reliable route out of intergroup conflict.
- **Triangulation** — two parties recruiting a third instead of addressing each other directly.
